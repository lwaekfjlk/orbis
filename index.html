MODEL NOTES / VERSION 6

Possibilities, not prophecies.

Strict causal order

Physical geography is frozen before people exist. Freshwater accessibility, cultivation potential, terrain cost and port suitability determine carrying capacity. Computational districts are NOT towns: many contain only dispersed households. Candidate market towns compete for a finite, non-duplicated share of hinterland food surplus over a pre-state land, lake and ocean graph. Salt water does not provide drinking-water access. Polities are selected from existing towns and institutions sampled afterwards. Two independent seeds separate natural geography from settlement history. The on-screen physical fingerprint detects any mutation of height, climate, water, ice and plate fields.

Initial rural and urban capacities are uncalibrated heuristics. Soil quality is a generated proxy; harbor shelter and rainwater collection are simplified. Lake transport uses same-basin adjacency; land costs use the district graph, not individually surveyed roads. Initial surplus sharing is conservative, but annual food trade remains the inherited aggregate realm-level model. Urban support corridors are fixed during history; no active water engineering, road building or new-town founding is simulated.

What the civilization simulation actually updates

Every year updates provincial population, development, food access and unrest; realm revenues, reserves, military forces, technology, magic and stability; diplomatic relations, trade and defensive pacts; local campaigns, territorial transfers, capital relocation, state elimination and possible secession. Annual random choices are seeded. Climate is fixed during the civilization timeline: history does not advance geological time.

Peoples, faiths and governments are different layers

Seven fictional peoples and six fictional traditions have mixed local population shares. Cultural homelands overlap and are not the same as political boundaries. Migration preserves population mixtures; neighboring contact gradually spreads faith. Conquest changes the owner and increases occupation unrest without automatically replacing inhabitants or converting them. Species have no fixed intelligence, moral alignment or combat bonus. State tradition is distinct from population belief. Homelands are seeded among already populated places and spread along the pre-state transport graph. Governments are sampled from options weighted by existing economic conditions and local beliefs. There is no required list of countries, magic states or desert kingdoms; geography does not uniquely determine institutions.

Geography and water

Continental provinces are design priors constrained by plate-boundary uplift and a coarse climate. Volcanic centers are spaced apart and only a small subset are visually active. Coastal troughs in cold high-relief regions are carved below sea level to represent a former glacial episode; this is a fjord heuristic, not an erosion history. Inland subsidence basins are additional explicit design priors. Priority-flood spill topology identifies depressions. Each significant basin stores water from catchment runoff and rainfall, loses evaporation and can overflow. A separate water surface is solved from storage and the basin's bed elevations. Rivers can terminate in inland lakes. Wetlands use low slope, water convergence, lake proximity and moisture; rainfall alone does not define them.

Trade and conflict constraints

At founding, existing towns retain or negotiate local autonomy. Larger nearby towns can attract minor centers, but there is no exclusion of all cities within a fixed radius and no quota per continent. A separate administrative graph measures land travel through the unchanged terrain. Political claims grow through held neighboring districts, pay a finite administrative upkeep cost, and cannot cross another capital. Trade routes do not directly spread sovereignty. Initial holdings are land-contiguous; later wars may create detached territories. Overseas routes are traced through connected ice-accessible ocean cells, never straight through continents. Wars need an existing frontier or sea contact, and naval campaigns need ships. Supply distance, terrain, capital defenses and bounded defensive-alliance support modify campaigns. Dashed diplomatic links show political relationships, not travel paths; the lighter sea lanes show computed ocean paths. Population, wealth and strength use uncalibrated model units. The simulation has no historical prediction claim.

Important limitations

Faith diffusion is primarily over neighboring provinces; it is not an individual-level social network. Trade volumes and food sharing are aggregate heuristics, not market-clearing economics. States cannot build canals or dynamically create new shipping corridors. Glacier melt is not yet fed into basin hydrology; lake evaporation is not fed back into atmospheric moisture. Basin water budgets conserve the model's storage units, but the time scale, cell areas and climate coefficients are not physically calibrated. A terminal-lake salinity label is a proxy, not a salt-transport simulation. Civilizations do not change terrain or climate. Country names come from existing town names; polity creation never relocates, renames or promotes a settlement. Initial state formation is a rule-based consolidation pass, not a fully time-integrated emergence of institutions.
